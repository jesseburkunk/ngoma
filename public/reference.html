Rhythm Reference

Every rhythm in Ngoma: how it sounds, how it is written and where it comes from, so you learn the rhythms while you jam. Each clip is eight bars from the app itself, synthesized, with the default kit, at a typical tempo for the style: once with the drums alone, once with pad, the soft layer in your key that Ngoma plays underneath. Read the grid from left to right; the gaps mark the beats. Open Ngoma to play with any of them, tune them to your key and export them.

West African

Kuku

4/4 · sixteenths120 BPM

Kuku comes from the Konianka people around Beyla and Nzérékoré in Guinea's forest region, where women danced it in a circle after returning from fishing. At first only djembes played it; the dunun parts came later. The conga plays the kenkeni on the first two pulses of each beat, the tumba plays the sangban, open on one, the and of two, three and the and of four, and the surdo plays the dununba.

Djole

4/4 · sixteenths124 BPM

Djole comes from a mask dance of the Temne people of Sierra Leone, in which a man carries a female mask. It was later arranged for djembe and dunun and became popular in Guinea. Baião comes from northeast Brazil and became popular across the country from the 1940s; its rhythm underlies forró. Samai 10/8

4/4 · sixteenths92 BPM

Sama'i thaqil is a 10/8 cycle used in the muwashshah song form and as the main rhythm of the Ottoman and Arabic instrumental sama'i. The ten beats are grouped 3+2+2+3: doum . . tek . doum doum tek . . with the weight on beats 1, 4, 6, 7 and 8, and a soft ka on ten leads back to the start. In the instrumental sama'i the last section usually switches to another meter. Each eighth is one sixteenth here, so the ten-step cycle moves against a 4/4 bar.

Aksak, Turkish for limping, is a nine-pulse meter grouped 2+2+2+3, common in Turkish and Balkan music. Dembow takes its name from a Jamaican dancehall riddim of around 1990. 4/4 · sixteenths112 BPM

Amapiano is South African house music that grew out of township music scenes in Gauteng, around Pretoria and Johannesburg, in the 2010s, usually at around 110 to 115 BPM. The shaker runs in sixteenths with accents on the offbeats. The conga plays on two, the last sixteenth of two, the and of three and four. Keherwa

4/4 · sixteenths92 BPM

Keherwa is an eight-matra taal in two halves of four, used in bhajans, qawwali, folk and Hindi film songs. The theka is dha ge na ti | na ka dhi na, at two sixteenths per matra. Listen for the bayan: it rings open on dha, ge and dhi and plays a closed ka in the second half, where the khali falls.

Dadra

12/8 · triplets80 BPM

Dadra is a six-matra taal split 3+3, standard for light classical and song forms such as dadra, ghazal and film songs. The theka is dha dhi na | dha ti na, with khali on the fourth matra. Pizzica

12/8 · triplets150 BPM

Pizzica comes from the Salento in southern Puglia. Its tarantata form was one of the musics associated with tarantism, a healing tradition for people, mostly women, believed bitten by the taranta spider, which Ernesto de Martino documented in 1959 (The Land of Remorse). This loop is a dance rhythm drawn from it. The tamburello splits each beat in three: an accented stroke on the skin, a flick with the back of the hand, then the fingertips on the jingles.

Origins and respect

Ngoma is a term found in many Bantu languages, and its meaning differs from place to place. It can mean a drum, but also music, dance and performance, and in some communities a healing practice or a ritual or social gathering (John M. Janzen, Ngoma: Discourses of Healing in Central and Southern Africa, 1992). Many of the rhythms in this tool belong to living traditions, passed on through communities, families, teachers and performers, often by listening, imitating and taking part (UNESCO describes flamenco this way, for example). They are offered here as starting points to learn from and build on, not as the real thing.

Based on, not the same as
Each preset is a simplified reading of one part of a much richer ensemble, squeezed into a loop. Some are checked against sources, others are reconstructed from general knowledge; the rhythm card says so when a rhythm is not or only partly checked. The North Indian presets keep only the theka: the characteristic basic pattern that articulates a tāl, the rhythmic cycle. In real tabla playing the theka is a framework for variation, improvisation and interaction. Real performances breathe, answer each other and change; a loop does not.
Sacred and ceremonial contexts
Some rhythms have a place in religious or ritual life. In Cuban Lucumí practice a bembé can be a religious drumming gathering in honour of the orishas; the word is also used more broadly for drumming gatherings and for certain drums, so the meaning depends on context. Soli is a family of Malinké (Maninka) rhythms associated in Guinea with initiation and circumcision ceremonies, with regional variants. Ayyub is an Arabic rhythm used in several contexts; in Egypt it is also associated with zār, a healing-ritual tradition. Many maracatu nação groups in Recife have historical and ongoing ties with Afro-Brazilian religious houses, including Candomblé, Jurema and Umbanda. Nyabinghi drumming is part of Rastafari ceremonial gatherings in Jamaica. Pizzica tarantata was one of the musics associated with tarantism, a healing tradition in the Salento that has since disappeared. Here you get only a groove derived from them, not the ceremony. Please name them with care.
Regions
The tabs are practical groupings for finding your way, not hard borders between traditions. Afro-Cuban: Cuba, where traditions of Yoruba, Kongo and Carabalí origin mixed with many others. West African: Mande drumming of Guinea and Mali, Temne drumming of Sierra Leone. Brazilian: Rio de Janeiro, Recife and the Northeast. Middle Eastern: Egypt, the Levant and Turkey, plus the aksak meters that Turkey shares with the Balkans. Caribbean: reggae, dub and Nyabinghi from Jamaica; dembow, which runs from Jamaican dancehall through Panama and Puerto Rico, with a major later development in the Dominican Republic; cumbia from the Caribbean coast of Colombia. South Africa: amapiano, rooted in Gauteng's township music scenes. North Indian: Hindustani tabla thekas, and bhangra, the Punjabi folk dance with the dhol, a separate tradition from the same region. Southern Europe: frame-drum traditions of southern Italy, including pizzica in Puglia and various tarantella traditions in Campania, each with its own local tamburello practices; and flamenco, whose heartland is Andalusia, with roots in Murcia and Extremadura too. Euclidean: some patterns can also be described with Godfried Toussaint's Euclidean rhythms, a mathematical way of distributing beats as evenly as possible. That is a way to describe them, not a claim that the traditions came from the algorithm. Hypno is different: style sketches from electronic music (halftime dubstep, two-step, hypnotic and minimal techno), not traditions.
Learn from the source
If a rhythm grabs you, find a teacher or a recording from the tradition itself. If you play in one of these traditions and hear something wrong here, corrections are very welcome.
