Ngoma is a loop tool for tuned world percussion: pick a rhythm and a key, press play, and the drums and a soft pad play together in tune. The pad keeps you in the loop while you tweak, so you stay with it longer. Export a seamless loop, stems and MIDI for your DAW. Every sound is synthesized in the browser, nothing is sampled.
The rhythms are inspired by living traditions from Cuba, West Africa, Brazil, the Middle East, the Caribbean, South Africa, North India and Southern Europe. They are simplified readings for a loop, not transcriptions: , and learn from players in those traditions. The rhythm reference lets you hear every rhythm and see how it is written.

Start here

Five steps, about two minutes. Everything is in tune, so you can't play a wrong note.

  1. Pick a rhythm. Choose a tradition in the tabs, then a rhythm. The card below it says where the rhythm comes from.
  2. Press Play (or the space bar). Set the tempo with BPM, or tap it in with Tap (key T).
  3. Tune it to your track. Pick Key and Scale and press Tune to key.
  4. Shape the groove. Turn Density, Variation and Polymeter, or press Suggest for three variations.
  5. Keep it. Store it in a Scene, or export the loop as WAV and MIDI.

Try: hold Drop (key D) for two bars and let go. The drums fall away while the bells keep time, then everything slams back in.

Play

Transport

Play · Refresh!
Play starts and stops. Refresh! starts a clean jam: everything back to how Ngoma first opens, while tempo, key and scenes stay.
MoreRefresh! resets rhythm, kit, groove, Pad, Lead, Magic and all effects. ← brings back what you had.
Drop · Drums off
Hold Drop (key D) for a breakdown: the drums drop out, bells, clave and shaker keep time. Drums off silences all drums until you press it again (key M).
← →
Step back and forth through your last 30 versions, like a browser.
MoreEvery randomize, new variant, Rnd, kit roll, reset and scene is kept as a version, so you can roll freely and return to the one that grooved.
Key · Scale · Tune to key
The key and scale of your track. Tune to key tunes the drums: low drums to the root, tumba and bongo to the fifth, djembe to the minor seventh. Lock to scale makes the Tune knobs land only on notes of the scale.
Chords · Per
One chord scheme for the whole piece. The pad, Worp, Resonate and the tuned drums follow it. Per sets how long each chord lasts.
MoreDrone (the default) is Ngoma as it was: no changes. Auto picks a scheme that suits the tradition: Afro-Cuban 1 · 4 · 5 · 4, Caribbean 1 · 4, Middle Eastern and Southern Europe 1 · 7 · 6 · 5; North Indian stays on the drone. Wander finds its own way and comes home now and then. Every chord is built from your scale, so whatever you play on top still fits; in a pentatonic scale many chords have no third and show as sus4. The tuned drums move with the chord and keep their place in it (the root drum lands on the new root, the fifth on the new fifth); shakers, triangle, tambourine and claps stay. Switch that off with Drums too. The scope at the top shows the chord now and the next one. Export a loop at least as long as the scheme to get all of it; Ngoma tells you when it is shorter. With Chords on, the export also has a MIDI file with the chords (three notes from your scale per chord), for a pad or piano in your DAW.
MIDI
Play along from a MIDI keyboard. Click MIDI: connect once; after that Ngoma connects by itself.
MoreLead or Pad picks what your keys play (the Worp pad; when the one you pick is off, the other plays). Tight makes every key wait for the next sixteenth while Ngoma plays, so what you play lands on the grid; a key just after the line plays at once.
Stable
Next to Volume. For a laptop where the sound stutters: a bigger audio buffer and a longer look-ahead, at the cost of a little more delay on MIDI keys. When the scope shows LATE, Ngoma could not keep up for a moment.
Volume
Master volume for listening, at the end of the scenes line. Double-click for full. Exports are always at -16 LUFS, whatever it says.

Scenes and Morph

Scenes 1 to 8
Eight slots that keep everything you hear. Click an empty slot to store, a stored one to play it on the next bar.
MoreA scene keeps rhythm, kit, groove, pad, effects and chords, not tempo or key. Shift-click or right-click stores over a slot, Alt-click clears it, keys 1 to 8 play them. Build an intro, a groove and a breakdown, then move between them.
Morph
Lets a scene grow out of what plays now, over 1 to 32 bars. Off switches on the next bar.
MoreThe knobs glide, the notes change one step at a time with the strong beats last, and the pad and anything that can't glide change halfway.

Try: store your groove in 1, mute two tracks and store that in 2. Set Morph to 8 and click 1 and 2 in turn.

Rhythm

Choosing a rhythm

Traditions and rhythms
Tabs for ten traditions, each with rhythms built from the parts each drum plays there. Hypno and Euclidean are style sketches, not traditions.
MoreOrigins at the top tells where each rhythm comes from, what it means in its own tradition and which sources it is based on. Every rhythm card links to its own sources.
Back to the written rhythm
Puts the rhythm back exactly as written, with the default groove. Your sound, effects, tempo, key and locked tracks stay.
MoreThe groove settings it resets: density, variation, swing and feel, humanize, life, polymeter, dialogue, fill, mutes and step settings. Clicking a rhythm chip again only restores its notes and keeps your settings, so you can keep playing.
Anchor
The timeline of the tradition, such as the clave or the bell. Suggest and Randomize leave it alone.
MoreClave or bell in Afro-Cuban music, bell and dununba in West Africa, surdo and agogô in Brazil, the darbuka rhythm in the Middle East, the dembow on cajón, the conga figure in amapiano, the bayan of the theka in North Indian taal. Euclidean rhythms have no anchor: lock a track to make it one. Rnd on the anchor itself still works.

Shaping the groove

Density
How many strokes play. 60% is the rhythm as written; lower thins out soft strokes first, higher adds ghost notes.
Variation
Changes that come and go, the way players vary. Hypnotic changes a note or two every fourth cycle, Loose varies more freely.
MoreThe home loop always comes back. The timeline never moves, and the anchor only gets soft grace notes. The other drums change where players change: on the pickup into a beat and towards the end of the phrase, not on the downbeat. Accents stay; soft strokes drop out, turn into another stroke or get a grace note, never right against another stroke. Low drums vary least.
Swing and feel
Delays the offbeats on tracks with Sw on. 58% is the default; 50% is straight, 66% a full triplet.
MoreClick Sw again for half. Swing 16th counts like an MPC, in the range Roger Linn describes (54% loosens a straight beat, 62% swings loosely around 90 BPM). Samba plays the third and fourth sixteenth early and the first slightly late, as measured in Brazilian samba (Naveda et al. 2011). Jembe (Mali) uses the short-medium-long subdivision measured in Malian jembe music (Polak 2010); with a template, 50% is the measured feel. When a swung and a straight track land on the same step, hits that would end up 8 to 35 ms apart (where the ear hears a sloppy flam) are pulled together. Real flams and rolls stay.
Humanize
Small, repeatable differences in timing and velocity on every hit.
Life
The drums you hear most swell and ease over a few bars, and a thin high track adds a soft three-against-four ghost line. 0 is the rhythm exactly as written.
Polymeter
Gives some tracks an odd length, so they drift against the bar. Low drums and clave stay anchored longest.
MoreShorter tracks cut the loop, longer ones keep cycling it. The orange badge on a track shows the length it plays. Between 25 and 45% grooves best; above 80% the groove loses its footing, good for a break.
Dialogue
Two players talk: a lead drum plays a short call at the end of one bar, another drum answers at the end of the next.
MoreBongo, djembe, darbuka or tabla lead, like the quinto over the other congas; the answer comes preferably from another register, with the same rhythm on its own strokes. While a player talks it leaves its own part out. The timeline, the anchor, shaker and bells never take part. Higher means more notes and more conversations.

Trying something new

Suggest
Makes 40 variations, scores them and offers the best three as A, B and C. Click one to hear it.
MoreThe score looks at syncopation (a medium amount grooves best, as Witek et al. 2014 found), whether the density is in the range of the presets, whether accents fight for the same register on the same step, and whether the loop still repeats itself. The three are chosen so they really differ.
★ Keep
Keeps the notes of this pattern as a favourite above the tracks. A favourite loads only its notes; × removes it.
Surprise · New variant
Surprise gives a new take on the rhythm. New variant makes one to three small changes per track; accents stay.
Randomize
New steps, pitch, velocity or all, on the tracks that play. Locked tracks never change.
MoreIt is not a coin toss: it weighs the metre, what each register usually does (low drums on strong beats and pickups, mid drums syncopated, high parts on the offbeats) and what the other tracks already play, and fills the gaps instead of doubling them.
Changes land on the bar
While Ngoma plays, a new rhythm or variation waits for the next bar line, so the groove never breaks. Knobs and sounds change at once.

Kit

Roll kit · Reset kit
Roll kit gives every unlocked drum a new tuning (inside the scale), decay, pan and tone. Dice amount sets how far. Reset kit brings back the default kit.

Tracks

Tracks are grouped low, mid and high, each with its own colour: green, ochre and blue. Only playing hides the tracks this rhythm does not use.

Reading a track

Steps
Click for a hit, again for the next stroke, once more to clear. Shift-click or right-click toggles accent and soft.
MoreThe letter shows the stroke and the shade tells them apart: full colour is open or bass, dark is mute or tone, light is slap or ka. A dashed outline marks a variation hit, a dot a step with settings.
Light
Lights up on every hit. Click it to mute the track.
M · S · bin
Mute, solo, and clear every note of the track (← brings them back).

Sound of a track

Level · Tune · Decay · Pan
Volume, pitch in semitones, length of the ring and stereo position.
MoreLevel goes past 100% into boost, up to 160%, and the ring turns red: for a surdo that has to work as a kick. Open and muted strokes sound on the note under the track name; slaps ring a third or fourth higher, always on a note of your scale.
Push
Moves the track up to 20 ms ahead of or behind the grid: a shaker slightly behind, a clave slightly ahead.
Var · ~ Drift
Var makes every hit a little different, as real drums do. ~ adds a slow wander of pitch, colour and level over a few bars.
MoreDrift is off by default and goes up to about 25 cents. While it plays, the bar under ~ sways and the note under the name shows the offset, like B1 +13¢.
Rev · Dly
How much of the track goes to the reverb and the delay.
⚄ dice
A new sound for this drum only: tuning, decay, colour, variation, pan and push. The rhythm stays.

Track options

Length · Sw · Lock · Rnd · E
Steps in the track (2 to 32), swing on, keep this track when you change rhythm, a new part for this track only, and the Euclidean generator.
Choke
A new stroke stops what is still ringing, as a hand damps the skin. A and B let tracks in a group cut each other.
MoreFor example conga and tumba played by one player, where a mute on one stops the open tone of the other. Flams and rolls are left alone.
Track controls
Opens or closes the knob row of every track. The round arrow next to a name folds one track.
Audition
Off by default. Switch it on to hear a drum when you turn its knobs, roll its dice or click a step while Ngoma is stopped. Clicking a track name always plays it.

Step settings

Pick an edit mode above the tracks, then click steps to cycle through values. Shift-click goes back, right-click resets.

Chance
100, 75, 50, 25 or 10% that the step plays each time.
Condition
1:2 plays on the first of every two cycles, 3:4 on the third of four. fill plays only in a fill, !fill only outside one.
Flam / roll
F adds a soft grace stroke just before the hit; R2 to R4 make a roll of two to four strokes.
Pitch · Decay
Tune or shorten just this one step, for melodic figures on conga, udu or talking drum.
Fill
Hold Fill for a fill, or let Auto fill play one every 4, 8 or 16 bars.

Layers

What plays with the drums, always in your key, scale and chords. Each layer has its own On switch.

Pad
A soft layer under the drums. Worp throws a new patch from Ngoma's sister synth; Classic has six pads of its own.
More

Worp: every throw is a patch of up to four sources with slow modulation that keeps re-patching itself. New throws another, the numbered buttons mute its layers, Open in Worp opens Worp over Ngoma while Ngoma keeps playing (Ngoma or Esc brings you back). Whatever you pick or change there plays here at once; ← undoes a new sound.

Classic types: Bed, one chord per bar. Loops, five tone fragments on their own lengths, like a Buddha Machine. Tides, a four-voice chord whose voices swell on their own cycles. Echoes, two short phrases into a darkening tape echo. Seasons and Cloud, a slow chord walk with shared notes held, as a warm pad or a cloud of grains.

Knobs: Level (0 is really off), Move (how far the filter breathes, or how deep a Worp gate dips), Filter (low-pass to the left, high-pass to the right), Reverb and Echo, and Pitch (up a fourth, a fifth or an octave). Off at the first start.

Lead
A struck or plucked melody from Worp, in time and with the swing of the drums. Off by default.
More

Every lead has one of nine ways of making its line, shown next to its code: Bounce (after Dillon Bastan's Bouncy Notes), Anchor (the rhythm of the tradition's timeline), Triplets, Loop, Phase (after Steve Reich), Modules (after Terry Riley's In C), Additive (after Philip Glass), Isorhythm and Pulse (after Music for 18 Musicians). The sounds: marimba, vibraphone, kalimba, balafon, soft electric piano, reed, celesta or music box.

Knobs: Level, Complexity (left repeats longer and stays on the grid, right moves on sooner and bounces freely), Filter, Reverb, Echo and Pitch. New sound throws another sound and keeps the melody, New pattern another melody on the same sound, Lock keeps the current bars exactly as they are.

Your own line: Rec 2 bars in Worp records one (a count-in, then two bars, quantized with the drums' swing). Mine | Worp switches between your line and Worp's.

Field
A field recording under the drums: a place rather than an instrument, in the spirit of Chris Watson and Jake Muir. Off by default.
More

Seven recordings by Félix Blume, all CC0 (public domain): a castle moat through hydrophones, a forest at night in Mexico, wind in a wire fence, dawn in the Amazon, ice on an alpine lake, a night in Port-au-Prince with the city far away, and a contact microphone on a bat nest. Your file… plays your own recording; it stays in this browser.

Distance pushes the place away: darker, quieter, more reverb. Drift lets the recording come apart into slow, overlapping grains at lower speeds, so it floats instead of running. Tune sends it through narrow filters on the tones of the current chord, so wind, water or insects start to sing the harmony. The low drums duck it a little. Everything is seeded, so the export sounds as you hear it, and loops without a seam.

Magic
Seven characters that work on the sound, not the rhythm. Amount says how far; everything stays in tune and in the export.
More

Breath: gentle life in timing and dynamics. Resonate: sympathetic strings on your key that ring with every stroke; Chord picks their notes (and with Chords on they follow the scheme). Comb: a tuned comb that moves to another note every two bars. Air: harmonics and sheen on top. Formant: two vowel filters, as if the kit speaks. Cosmos: a drifting echo after the SOMA Cosmos, two echo lines that drift apart and meet again. Ghosts: a tiny grain of a hit comes back a few steps later, quiet and far left or right.

Decay sets how long Resonate rings and Comb and Cosmos repeat. Every sends only every 2nd to 8th hit into Magic; the rest stays dry. Echo gives Magic its own dub echo. With Ghosts, Chance sets how often and Hard how present.

Studio

The units work like hardware: drag a knob up or down, hold Shift for fine moves, double-click for the default. The light by a name shows whether the unit is on.

Space

Reverb
Room, Plate, Hall, Cave, Spring and Bloom, with length, pre-delay, tone and return. The low end stays out. Bloom lets every hit open up into the chord.
About BloomA long tail that climbs an octave with every pass, like a shimmer reverb, and rings on the tones of the current chord, so the drums turn into a soft chord that swells and fades. It follows Chords; on Drone it rings on the root chord of your key. Shimmer (only with Bloom) sets how far the tail climbs: off keeps just the chord tones, higher lets it rise further. Works well with Resonate, and with Return around 20 to 40%.
MoreBuilt from a generated stereo impulse response: early reflections, density that builds up and highs that die faster than lows. Spring is a two-spring tank as in dub: every pass comes back as a small metallic chirp. Everything below 250 Hz is cut before the reverb. Every works as in Magic.
Delay
Synced to the tempo, 1/16 to 1/2. Clean or Tape echo; Send sets how much of the whole kit goes in.
MoreClean plays ping-pong, stereo or mono. Tape echo is modelled after the Roland RE-201 Space Echo: three heads (Heads), a warm tone, wow and flutter, and saturation in the feedback, so high Feedback sings instead of blowing up. Animate lets feedback and tone drift in a cycle as long as the loop, so exports stay seamless. Below 300 Hz is cut.

Tone

Low · Mid · High
A simple master EQ. High starts at -2 dB, where long sessions tire the ear first.
MoreA low shelf at 150 Hz, a bell at 1.2 kHz where the skin tones sit and a high shelf at 4 kHz.
Filter: Clean · Ladder · MS · SEM
A low-pass over the whole mix, in four classic characters. Cutoff, Reso and Drive set it.
MoreClean: transparent. Ladder: after the Moog ladder, 24 dB, round and warm. MS: after the Korg MS-20, 12 dB, keeps the bass and bites, screams at high resonance. SEM: after the Oberheim SEM, 12 dB, soft and open. Drive goes up to +18 dB with the level compensated, so louder does not fool your ear.
Move: Env · LFO · Rate
Makes the filter move: Env opens it on every hit, the LFO sweeps it in time with the loop.
MoreRate is once per loop, bar, half bar or beat, so the export stays seamless; Sync switches to a free speed in Hz. Env from picks what opens the filter: the whole mix, or one track as a sidechain. A dot on the Cutoff knob shows where the filter is right now. Ngoma starts with the filter breathing lightly.
Master · Width · Room
The finishing chain: glue, a light tape saturation, the lows in mono, a little width and air. Loudness stays about the same, so On and Off compare fairly.
MoreWidth spreads the players over the stereo field (0% is mono). Room puts the drums in a small wooden room, early reflections only; it is not the reverb. Stems skip Room and Master.
Weight · Kit pitch
Weight adds body and saturation inside every drum (25% by default). Kit pitch moves every drum up or down in semitones.
Flat
EQ flat, filter open, no drive, kit pitch 0, Master off and Weight 10%.

Try: filter MS, cutoff around 800 Hz, Reso 50%, Env 60%. Then set Env from to the conga.

Export

A loop

Export loop
A seamless WAV of 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars, plus MIDI and the pattern. Every export lands at -16 LUFS, peaks at least 1 dB under full scale.
MoreReverb and delay tails fold back to the start, so the loop sounds as if it has been running forever. With effects on you also get a dry WAV. JSON keeps the full pattern and settings.
Stems
Every playing track as its own WAV as well, aligned with the mix, so they add up to it.
Leave room for kick
Leaves low strokes on the beat out of the export, so your own kick stays alone there. You still hear everything while you play.

What you just played

Capture
Keeps the last 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars exactly as you heard them, Magic accidents and knob moves included. Key C.
MoreExport renders the pattern afresh; Capture keeps the take. You get a WAV (cut on the bar line, seamless) and a MIDI file. Works while playing and right after Stop. Drag take drags the WAV straight into your DAW.

Everything you played since Play, up to about 100 seconds, as WAV plus MIDI. Key Shift+C.
● Rec

Tips

  • Fold what you don't use. The arrow on a panel folds it away. A new session opens them all again, so nothing gets lost.
  • Registers. Low gives weight, mid speaks the language of the rhythm, high gives drive. Don't let all three play busy at once.
  • One change at a time. Hypnotic grooves come from repetition with small shifts. Try Variation on Hypnotic around 30% and leave it.
  • Accents make the groove. The bells accent the main beats and play the strokes between softer, as players do.
  • Clay and wood. Udu, cajón, bendir and talking drum sit outside the traditional rhythms. Try Clay & wood, or add them with Rnd.
  • Euclidean. 3 over 8 is the tresillo, 5 over 8 the cinquillo, 7 over 12 the West African bell, 4 over 9 the Turkish aksak (Toussaint).
  • Odd meters. Samai 10/8, Aksak 9/8 and Rupak (7 beats) run over the 4/4 grid, so they drift across the bar like a polymeter.
  • Always at hand. Scroll down and a slim bar at the top keeps Play, tempo, Capture, Drop and your scenes in reach.

Where the rhythms come from and the sources behind them: .

Origins: where the rhythms come from

Origins and respect

Ngoma is a term found in many Bantu languages, and its meaning differs from place to place. It can mean a drum, but also music, dance and performance, and in some communities a healing practice or a ritual or social gathering (John M. Janzen, Ngoma: Discourses of Healing in Central and Southern Africa, 1992). Many of the rhythms in this tool belong to living traditions, passed on through communities, families, teachers and performers, often by listening, imitating and taking part (UNESCO describes flamenco this way, for example). They are offered here as starting points to learn from and build on, not as the real thing.

Based on, not the same as
Each preset is a simplified reading of one part of a much richer ensemble, squeezed into a loop. Some are checked against sources, others are reconstructed from general knowledge; the rhythm card says so when a rhythm is not or only partly checked. The North Indian presets keep only the theka: the characteristic basic pattern that articulates a tāl, the rhythmic cycle. In real tabla playing the theka is a framework for variation, improvisation and interaction. Real performances breathe, answer each other and change; a loop does not.
Sacred and ceremonial contexts
Some rhythms have a place in religious or ritual life. In Cuban Lucumí practice a bembé can be a religious drumming gathering in honour of the orishas; the word is also used more broadly for drumming gatherings and for certain drums, so the meaning depends on context. Soli is a family of Malinké (Maninka) rhythms associated in Guinea with initiation and circumcision ceremonies, with regional variants. Ayyub is an Arabic rhythm used in several contexts; in Egypt it is also associated with zār, a healing-ritual tradition. Many maracatu nação groups in Recife have historical and ongoing ties with Afro-Brazilian religious houses, including Candomblé, Jurema and Umbanda. Nyabinghi drumming is part of Rastafari ceremonial gatherings in Jamaica. Pizzica tarantata was one of the musics associated with tarantism, a healing tradition in the Salento that has since disappeared. Here you get only a groove derived from them, not the ceremony. Please name them with care.
Regions
The tabs are practical groupings for finding your way, not hard borders between traditions. Afro-Cuban: Cuba, where traditions of Yoruba, Kongo and Carabalí origin mixed with many others. West African: Mande drumming of Guinea and Mali, Temne drumming of Sierra Leone, Ga (kpanlogo) and Ewe (agbekor) drumming of Ghana and Togo. Brazilian: Rio de Janeiro, Recife and the Northeast. Middle Eastern: Egypt, the Levant and Turkey, plus the aksak meters that Turkey shares with the Balkans. Caribbean: reggae, dub and Nyabinghi from Jamaica; dembow, which runs from Jamaican dancehall through Panama and Puerto Rico, with a major later development in the Dominican Republic; cumbia from the Caribbean coast of Colombia. Central & South Africa: amapiano, rooted in Gauteng's township music scenes, and cavacha, the drum rhythm of Congolese rumba and soukous from Kinshasa. North Indian: Hindustani tabla thekas, and bhangra, the Punjabi folk dance with the dhol, a separate tradition from the same region. Southern Europe: frame-drum traditions of southern Italy, including pizzica in Puglia and various tarantella traditions in Campania, each with its own local tamburello practices; and flamenco, whose heartland is Andalusia, with roots in Murcia and Extremadura too. Euclidean: some patterns can also be described with Godfried Toussaint's Euclidean rhythms, a mathematical way of distributing beats as evenly as possible. That is a way to describe them, not a claim that the traditions came from the algorithm. Hypno is different: style sketches from electronic music (halftime dubstep, two-step, hypnotic and minimal techno), not traditions.
Learn from the source
If a rhythm grabs you, find a teacher or a recording from the tradition itself. If you play in one of these traditions and hear something wrong here, corrections are very welcome.
